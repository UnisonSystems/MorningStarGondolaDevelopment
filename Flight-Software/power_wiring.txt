I'm designing a trajectory-prediction tool for high-altitude balloons (HAB). I'm starting from first principles where possible. For example, from Newton II, we have
$$F = m\vec{a} = (\rho_{\text{atm}}V - m)g\hat z - \frac{1}{2}\rho_{\text{atm}}C_DA|\vec{v}-\vec{u}|(\vec{v}-\vec{u})$$
Going through the right-hand side term-by-term...
$\rho_{\text{atm}}$, the atmospheric density, is well known as a function of altitude.
$V$, the balloon volume, is more difficult to predict, since it is driven by the pressure differential across the inflated balloon envelope, which subsequently depends on (1) the total amount of lifting gas in the balloon and (2) the elastic properties of the envelope—both quantities which are not easy to obtain.
$m$, the total system mass, is easier than $V$, but still presents difficulties. In real-world applications, $m$ is a composite of masses (e.g., payload(s), rigging, balloon envelope, lifting gas). As with $V$, the mass of the lifting gas is not easily obtained.
$g$ is the gravitational acceleration, which is extremely well known, and constant for all intents and purposes in the context of HABs.
$C_D$ is a bit hand-wavy, but probably use 0.25–0.3.
$A$, the reference area, is just $V$ with some extra stuff. Assuming a spherical balloon, $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\rightarrow R = \left[\frac{3V}{4\pi}\right]^{1/3}$, and $A = \pi R^2$. So, if you solve the $V$ problem, $A$ is likewise solved.
$\vec{v}$ is the velocity of the balloon. This velocity (and, necessarily, its magnitude) will come from integration of $\vec{a}$.
$\vec{u}$ is the local wind velocity (local implies "arbitrary point"). The atmosphere is extremely dynamic/chaotic and, of course, spans the entire planet up to hundreds of km. We rely on NOAA tools for this.
